# Zenith Capital Group — Discovery Call Playbook

Purpose: turn a positive reply into a clear understanding of a real operational problem before discussing software, price, or a proposal.

## Core Rule

Do not sell a predetermined app. Diagnose the workflow first.

A successful discovery conversation ends with enough information to answer:

- What is the current process?
- Who does the work?
- How often does it happen?
- How much time does it consume?
- What errors, delays, or frustration does it create?
- What tools are being used now?
- Why has the problem not already been fixed?
- What would a useful improvement look like?
- Is the problem valuable enough to justify a small paid project?

## Opening

Keep the tone conversational and low-pressure.

Suggested opening:

> Thanks for getting back to me. I'm not coming in with a canned product. I'm trying to understand where smaller operations are still losing time to manual processes and see whether there is a practical problem I can actually help solve. Can you walk me through the workflow you had in mind?

## Discovery Questions

Do not mechanically ask every question. Follow the conversation and go deeper where the pain is real.

1. **Walk me through how this works today.**
2. **Who touches this process from start to finish?**
3. **How often do you have to do it?**
4. **Where does it usually slow down or become annoying?**
5. **How much time would you estimate the team spends on it in a normal week or month?**
6. **What are you using now — spreadsheets, paper, email, an ERP, shared folders, something else?**
7. **What happens when something goes wrong or information is missing?**
8. **If this were working the way you wanted, what would be different?**
9. **Have you tried to fix it before? If so, what got in the way?**
10. **If there were a small, low-risk way to improve one part of this first, which part would matter most?**

## Signals of a Good First Project

Look for problems that are:

- repeated frequently;
- currently handled manually or with fragile spreadsheets/files;
- easy to observe and understand;
- painful enough that the customer already complains about them;
- narrow enough to improve without replacing the company's entire software stack;
- measurable in time saved, errors reduced, visibility improved, or delays removed;
- owned by a person who can approve a small project.

Strong examples include repetitive reporting, duplicate data entry, inventory/material tracking, file/CSV manipulation, status tracking, recurring spreadsheet cleanup, document handoffs, and lightweight internal dashboards/tools.

## Red Flags

Do not rush into a project when:

- the prospect cannot identify a concrete problem;
- the request is really a full ERP replacement;
- multiple departments must agree before anything can happen;
- success cannot be defined;
- the customer expects unlimited custom software for a tiny budget;
- the process changes constantly and nobody owns it;
- the project requires access, expertise, compliance, or security obligations Zenith is not prepared to handle.

## When They Describe a Real Pain

Do not immediately say, "I can build that."

First summarize it back:

> So if I'm understanding you correctly, right now [person/team] has to [manual process] about [frequency], and the biggest issue is [time/error/delay/visibility problem]. You'd ideally want [desired outcome]. Is that accurate?

If they agree, clarify any missing details and then say:

> That sounds like something worth scoping. I'd rather start with a small improvement we can prove than make this into a giant software project. Let me map out the simplest version that could produce a useful result and come back with a scope.

## Do Not Price Live Unless the Scope Is Obvious

For the first few Zenith engagements, prefer taking the problem away and returning with a written scope rather than improvising a price during discovery.

Before pricing, define:

- problem statement;
- current process;
- proposed first improvement;
- deliverables;
- customer responsibilities/access needed;
- exclusions;
- success criteria;
- estimated timeline;
- fixed price or clearly bounded pricing structure.

## First-Proposal Philosophy

The goal is not maximum contract value. The goal is the first useful paid proof.

Prefer a project that can become:

**Problem → small paid implementation → measurable result → testimonial/referral → reusable case study/module → larger or recurring work.**

## After the Call

Immediately record:

- date and participants;
- exact pain described;
- current workflow;
- frequency/time/cost indicators;
- existing tools;
- desired outcome;
- decision-maker and other stakeholders;
- urgency/timing;
- promised next action;
- next follow-up date.

Update the prospect status to **Discovery** when a real operational problem has been discussed.

## Zenith's Positioning During Discovery

Zenith helps small manufacturers, warehouses, distributors, and industrial businesses reduce repetitive reporting, inventory, data-entry, tracking, and workflow work with practical software and automation.

The technology is the implementation. The product being sold is the removal of an operational headache.
